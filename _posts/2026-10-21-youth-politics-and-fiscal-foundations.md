Gen Z has a lot of grievances when it comes to politics and fiscal foundations, but its grasp of the actual economics behind them is partial, and where understanding is partial, demands run past what actual economics allows.

Rents are too high, public services are strained, and young people's prospects feel narrower than their parents'. None of that is in actual dispute. The argument is about what comes next. Debt service, deficits, housing supply and incentives are intricate mechanisms, and the channels young people rely on reward outrage over detail. A movement that cannot price its own demands cannot hold the governments it elects to account, and the bill lands on the same young people.

This essay makes three core claims. First, France shows the math behind this: a debt of nearly 119% of GDP and a deficit above 5% collide with platforms that cancel debt and lower the retirement age. Second, the information environment keeps this understanding shallow. Third, the economic record favors market-based systems, so the housing and cost-of-living anger driving youth politics is better answered with supply and sound public finances than with abandoning markets.

Two limits matter. The claim is about understanding, not intelligence or motive, and the protests reviewed here are not trying to abolish capitalism. Also, direct evidence of what young people know about macroeconomics is thin, so I say explicitly where I'm arguing from indirect evidence.

France's debt is heading to 119.3% of GDP in 2026 and 121.7% in 2027, with a deficit of 5.4% of GDP against an EU limit of 3%. Higher interest rates make that debt costlier to carry each year, leaving less room for the schools and services protesters want funded. Against that, the leading figure of the French left campaigns on reducing the national debt and lowering the retirement age from 62 to 60. Cancelling the debt held by the Bank of France would move obligations between public bodies without removing them, and would signal to lenders that France may not honor its debts, which raises borrowing costs (more on this below). Lowering the retirement age adds pension spending while the deficit is already above 5%. These are central mechanisms of the debt problem, and a movement that does not engage with them is not engaging with the problem.

Additionally, the left's position is essentially redundant, as lowering the retirement age and eliminating national debt have zero correlation, since one adds spending and the other raises borrowing costs. Each attempt to narrow the deficit has met mass resistance, and the ratio keeps climbing. Amundi warns that without deficit reduction measures, debt could exceed 130% of GDP by 2030. In fairness, Amundi also judges debt sustainable over the medium term, and the school protests answer a real problem. The point is that such demands are rarely set against the deficit.

## Cancelling the debt

The French left's proposal is very specific. Mélenchon wants to cancel 18% of France's debt, the share he says the Bank of France holds, to free fiscal space for social spending, which would help to appease protests. It sounds like free money, so what could go wrong?

First, it does not remove the obligation. The Bank of France is part of the public sector, similar to the Federal Reserve. Cancelling what the state owes moves its liabilities from one public body to another, and it would breach the EU treaty article that bars central banks from financing governments. Second, major lenders respond to the signal. Creditors punish a borrower that refuses to pay by charging significantly higher interest rates when it seeks new financing. France's prime minister made the point in August that even entertaining the idea could spook investors while France needs to borrow record sums. Third, France is exposed to the response. Around half of French government debt is held by foreign investors, a higher share than in Italy, the US, Spain or Germany, all similarly indebted countries.

The chain is simple. The debt ratio is debt divided by GDP. If investors price in the risk of cancellation, borrowing costs rise, the interest bill grows, the deficit widens and the ratio goes up. A plan sold as lowering the ratio can actually raise it. Granted, the realistic goal is stabilizing the ratio, not eliminating debt. The government says it can stabilize once the deficit falls to 3% of GDP, and a target has been set for 2029. Mélenchon's package of promises is a hit among voters, but these policies clearly need to be analyzed further.

It is also worth noting that France is in a terrible storm in the bond market. The 10-year OAT has surged toward 5%, forcing France to pay much higher borrowing costs than historically riskier countries like Italy and Greece. Money that the left wants to go to education is swallowed up by interest payments projected to rise by about 59% by the end of the decade, right when the deficit target is supposed to be hit. That is not a good combination. France's total pension spending is 14% of GDP, which is absurd, and lowering the retirement age will pull more money from social welfare and raise the debt.

The easy way to secure more funding for pensions and education, the things the protests are centered on, is to issue more bonds and increase France's debt. That is the easy way out, and it goes against the policy I'm arguing for. France can't devalue or set its own rates, so adjustment has to come through taxes and spending rather than central policy, like a forced recession. France is essentially in a terrible situation.

## The information problem

More than 70% of French 18 to 29 year olds now get their news through social networks, and a majority say they struggle to rank what matters. The format is short, personal and emotional, which can lead to excessive passion. It is good at telling people what to feel about rents and schools, and poor at conveying how a deficit compounds or why cancelling debt backfires. A feed rewards the claim that fits in fifteen seconds, and the macroeconomics of public finance do not.

The direct evidence on what young people know is thin. A Wharton paper on US data found that only 27% of young adults have basic knowledge of interest rates, inflation and risk. In the OECD's 2022 test, 18% of 15 year olds on average lacked baseline financial literacy. A 2010 Pew quiz found older Americans did significantly better than the young on politics and economics questions. None of this measures French macroeconomic knowledge, and the studies are either dated or American, but the inferences drawn from them can't be ignored. Younger generations lack financial knowledge that affects their day-to-day decisions, and the same is happening in Spain.

## Spain, and the rent ceiling

Gen Z in Spain is clear on what they oppose, but vague on what replaces it. Bloomberg Economics ties the Gen Z protests to soaring rents, living costs and fears that AI will take jobs. The Journal of Democracy reads them as mainly anti-corruption. Nepal's protests toppled its government and Madagascar's president dissolved the government. But removing a government is not the same as building institutions that attract investment and keep markets working, and whether these uprisings produce them is still unsettled.

Spain has the same shape from the other direction. Housing is the country's top problem at 28.3% in CIS polling, and Vox, the party on the right, is reported as the most popular party among 18 to 34 year olds. Different poles, similar frustration, and in both cases the suggested remedy is more of a slogan than a mechanism.

A rent ceiling is the obvious proposed mechanism, but why not a general rent ceiling? A ceiling set below the market rent (the equilibrium) makes more people want apartments than landlords are willing to offer. The gap is a shortage, and because price can no longer ration the scarce homes, something else does: waiting lists, connections, and landlords choosing tenants.

At the ceiling, landlords offer the number of homes at the left dot while tenants want the number at the right dot, and the gap between them is the shortage. The shortage then grows, because landlords can leave the rental market. In San Francisco, a 1994 expansion of rent control limited renters' mobility by 20%, while the landlords covered by it cut their rental housing supply by 15%, by selling to owner-occupants and redeveloping. The authors conclude that the rules protected tenants in the short run, but the lost supply likely pushed market rents up in the long run, undermining the law's own goals. The people who gain are those already in homes. The people who pay are newcomers to the rental market, and young people are disproportionately newcomers.

{% include rent-supply-loss.html %}

The strongest objection comes from Spain, and the evidence there is mixed. A study of Catalonia's 2020 policy found that rents paid fell by about 4% to 6% with no evidence of reduced supply. Under the 2024 cap, new rents in regulated municipalities are 1.28% below their level at the start, against 9.52% higher elsewhere in Catalonia. Yet a 2025 evaluation of that cap reports that, in its analysis, the estimated price effect largely disappears while the reduction in rental supply remains robust, as landlords use seasonal rentals to sidestep the cap.

The defensible claim is much narrower than "rent control never lowers a rent." It is that a ceiling can lower measured rents for covered homes in the short run, at the cost of supply and of newcomers, and that it does nothing about the shortage that caused the high rents. Two economists who studied the evidence favor alternatives such as funded rent insurance, which helps renters in distress without distorting the market. Personally, I think that is the best fix. The durable fix is more homes, which is the point the Spanish voters who blame scarce construction are making.

## Price it before you demand it

The grievances behind these movements are real. Rents are too high, schools are strained, and many young people have good reason to feel the system is not working for them. But anger is not analysis, and the remedies on offer often do not survive contact with the numbers. Cancelling debt held by the central bank moves obligations around without removing them, and it raises borrowing costs for everyone, including the schools and services protesters want funded. Rent ceilings can protect people who are already housed while shrinking the supply newcomers need. Lowering the retirement age adds to a pension bill already at 14% of GDP in a country running a deficit above 5%.

None of this means young people should stop caring or stop speaking up. It means demands should be priced before they are made: what a policy costs, who pays, and how lenders and landlords will respond. The alternative to slogans is not silence. It is more homes, a gradual path to a sustainable deficit, and public services funded in a way that does not hand the bill to the next generation.

I make this argument as a member of Gen Z. The direct evidence on how much young people understand is thin, and I have argued from indirect evidence where I had to. But the mechanisms themselves are not in doubt, and understanding them is the best way to turn legitimate frustration into lasting change.
